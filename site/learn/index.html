Why the Greasiest Drug Isn't the Best One

Make a drug more oily and it often gets more potent. Keep going and the potency falls again. Three small simulations show why: a drug has to dissolve in oil to cross a cell membrane, and then it has to leave the oil again.

Oil or water?

Shake a drug with two liquids that don't mix, octanol (an oily alcohol) on top and water below, and let it settle. The drug molecules keep hopping across the boundary in both directions. They end up split between the layers in a fixed ratio, called the partition coefficient P. Chemists use its logarithm, logP. Octanol is the standard stand-in for the fatty inside of a cell membrane.

Each step of 1 in logP is a tenfold change in preference. At logP 3 a drug prefers oil a thousand to one, and at −1 it prefers water ten to one. That makes logP a single number for how greasy a molecule is.

A drug has to swim, then squeeze through

A swallowed pill dissolves in the water of the gut. To reach its receptor it has to cross the gut wall into the blood, then cross another barrier into the tissue where the receptor sits. Each barrier is a cell membrane: a sheet of fat two molecules thick. A molecule gets through by dissolving into the fat and then leaving it on the far side, so it moves from water to fat to water, twice.

Below, the chance of a molecule stepping from water into fat, or from fat back into water, follows its logP, just as in the beaker. Then a clock runs and we count how many reach the receptor before time is up.

Both ends fail for opposite reasons. A water-loving drug rarely enters a membrane. A very greasy drug enters the first membrane easily but rarely leaves it. The best drugs sit in between, where the step in and the step out are about equally likely.

Why Hansch's straight lines bend

In 1964 Corwin Hansch and Toshio Fujita proposed that you could predict how potent each member of a family of related drugs would be from a few numbers, logP among them. Potency is written as log(1/C), where C is the dose needed for a fixed effect, so bigger means more potent. In its simplest form the equation is a straight line:

log(1/C) = a·logP + c

The trouble is that a straight line can't rise and then fall. Below, the hidden "truth" is the membrane model from the figure above: potency is the log of the share of the dose that reaches the receptor in time. A series of seven related drugs samples one slice of it, and we fit an equation to those seven points, as a chemist would.

Where the series sits decides what the chemist sees. A series of water-loving compounds gives a clean rising line, so "more greasy, more potent" looks like a rule. A series of greasy compounds gives a falling line. Only a series that spans the optimum shows the bend, and only a curved equation can describe it. In 1968 Hansch added a squared term, giving the parabola log(1/C) = −a·(logP)² + b·logP + c with its peak at logP₀ = b/2a. In 1977 Hugo Kubinyi showed that a "bilinear" shape, straight on both sides with a rounded top, fits real series better. That is the shape this membrane model produces.

An explorable explanation. The figures are live simulations running in your browser.

  • Simplified and illustrative. The beaker has equal volumes of the two liquids. The membrane model has two membranes, one hopping rate (k = 1 per time unit), a membrane that holds β = 0.01 of a water compartment's volume and a fixed measuring time (5 units). These numbers were chosen for illustration and are not fitted to any drug. The optimum at logP ≈ 2 comes from β (it sits at logP = −log β), so a different body or assay would move it. Potency in the third figure is relative, with an arbitrary offset.
  • The membrane model. Molecules step from water into fat at rate k·βP/(1 + βP) and from fat into water at k/(1 + βP), so the fat-to-water concentration ratio at equilibrium is P. The receptor compartment keeps any molecule that arrives. The share reaching it is computed by integrating the rate equations (RK4), and the moving dots are one random run of the same rates. This follows Penniston, Beckett, Bentley and Hansch (1969); its shape is Kubinyi's bilinear equation.
