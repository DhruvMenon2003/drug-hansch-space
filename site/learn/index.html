Why the Greasiest Drug Isn't the Best One

Make a drug more oily and it often gets more potent. Keep going and the potency falls again. Three small simulations show why: a drug has to dissolve in oil to cross a cell membrane, and then it has to leave the oil again. Three more show what else the equation needs, how it does on this site's real drugs, and how to tell a real fit from a lucky one.

Oil or water?

Shake a drug with two liquids that don't mix, octanol (an oily alcohol) on top and water below, and let it settle. The drug molecules keep hopping across the boundary in both directions. They end up split between the layers in a fixed ratio, called the partition coefficient P. Chemists use its logarithm, logP. Octanol is the standard stand-in for the fatty inside of a cell membrane.

Each step of 1 in logP is a tenfold change in preference. At logP 3 a drug prefers oil a thousand to one, and at −1 it prefers water ten to one. That makes logP a single number for how greasy a molecule is.

A drug has to swim, then squeeze through

A swallowed pill dissolves in the water of the gut. To reach its receptor it has to cross the gut wall into the blood, then cross another barrier into the tissue where the receptor sits. Each barrier is a cell membrane: a sheet of fat two molecules thick. A molecule gets through by dissolving into the fat and then leaving it on the far side, so it moves from water to fat to water, twice.

Below, the chance of a molecule stepping from water into fat, or from fat back into water, follows its logP, just as in the beaker. Then a clock runs and we count how many reach the receptor before time is up.

Both ends fail for opposite reasons. A water-loving drug rarely enters a membrane. A very greasy drug enters the first membrane easily but rarely leaves it. The best drugs sit in between, where the step in and the step out are about equally likely.

Why Hansch's straight lines bend

In 1964 Corwin Hansch and Toshio Fujita proposed that you could predict how potent each member of a family of related drugs would be from a few numbers, logP among them. Potency is written as log(1/C), where C is the dose needed for a fixed effect, so bigger means more potent. In its simplest form the equation is a straight line:

log(1/C) = a·logP + c

The trouble is that a straight line can't rise and then fall. Below, the hidden "truth" is the membrane model from the figure above: potency is the log of the share of the dose that reaches the receptor in time. A series of seven related drugs samples one slice of it, and we fit an equation to those seven points, as a chemist would.

Where the series sits decides what the chemist sees. A series of water-loving compounds gives a clean rising line, so "more greasy, more potent" looks like a rule. A series of greasy compounds gives a falling line. Only a series that spans the optimum shows the bend, and only a curved equation can describe it. In 1968 Hansch added a squared term, giving the parabola log(1/C) = −a·(logP)² + b·logP + c with its peak at logP₀ = b/2a. In 1977 Hugo Kubinyi showed that a "bilinear" shape, straight on both sides with a rounded top, fits real series better. That is the shape this membrane model produces.

Grease isn't the only thing that matters

Getting to the receptor is half the story. Once there, the drug has to bind, and binding cares about more than grease. Hansch's full equation describes a family of drugs that share one parent structure and differ by one small group, the substituent, swapped onto the same spot. Each substituent gets three numbers. π is how much it adds to logP. σ, from Louis Hammett's work in 1937, is how strongly it pulls electrons towards itself (positive) or pushes them away (negative). MR, molar refractivity, measures how bulky it is.

log(1/C) = a·π + b·σ + c·MR + d

Below are 17 analogues of one imaginary drug, each with a different group on the para position of a benzene ring, using the real π, σ and MR of that group. Their potency comes from a hidden rule: grease helps, a bulky group doesn't fit the pocket, and electron pull matters as much as the slider says. You choose which terms the fitted equation may use. If the equation is right, every dot lands on the diagonal, where predicted equals observed.

This is why Hansch analysis worked so well in the 1960s and 1970s: within one tight family of compounds, a handful of physical numbers explained most of the potency. It also shows what the method needs. The compounds must share a parent structure and bind the same way, so that the substituent is the only thing that changes.

Now try it on real drugs

This site's explorer has a potency value for 308 of its drugs: the median ChEMBL pChEMBL at each drug's main target, kept only when at least two independent assays agree. pChEMBL is −log of the molar IC50, Ki or similar, so it is a log(1/C) in Hansch's sense. Drugs that hit the same target form a group. The descriptors are the explorer's own: Crippen logP, MR and the polar surface area TPSA, all from RDKit.

Real drugs that share a target are rarely one family. An SSRI and a tricyclic antidepressant both block the serotonin transporter with completely different skeletons. So expect weaker fits than in the figure above, and expect a new problem. With eight drugs and two terms, a line can fit well by luck. To check, we also report q²: leave one drug out, refit, predict the drug you left out, and repeat for every drug. q² is r² computed on those honest predictions. If q² is far below r², the equation is memorising, not predicting.

A few groups give a usable fit, such as the mu opioid antagonists on logP or the beta-2 agonists on TPSA. Most do not, and adding terms rarely helps once q² is checked. That is not a flaw in Hansch's idea. It is a sign that these groups break its assumption of one family binding one way. The explorer's fits are labelled exploratory for the same reason.

More terms always fit better, which is the trap

Every extra term in a least-squares fit can only raise r², even a term that means nothing. In 1972 John Topliss and Robert Costello showed how easily this fools people: with enough candidate descriptors, random numbers alone produce impressive r² values. Below, twelve of the analogues from before are fitted with π and σ, which really do drive their potency, plus as many columns of pure random numbers as you like.

The usual rule of thumb is at least five compounds for every term in the equation, and a q² that stays close to r². The other check is y-randomisation: shuffle the potencies, refit, and make sure the real fit beats the shuffled ones. The explorer runs both checks on every group it fits, which is why most of its real-drug fits are labelled exploratory.

  • Simplified and illustrative. The beaker has equal volumes of the two liquids. The membrane model has two membranes, one hopping rate (k = 1 per time unit), a membrane that holds β = 0.01 of a water compartment's volume and a fixed measuring time (5 units). These numbers were chosen for illustration and are not fitted to any drug. The optimum at logP ≈ 2 comes from β (it sits at logP = −log β), so a different body or assay would move it. Potency in the third figure is relative, with an arbitrary offset.
  • The membrane model. Molecules step from water into fat at rate k·βP/(1 + βP) and from fat into water at k/(1 + βP), so the fat-to-water concentration ratio at equilibrium is P. The receptor compartment keeps any molecule that arrives. The share reaching it is computed by integrating the rate equations (RK4), and the moving dots are one random run of the same rates. This follows Penniston, Beckett, Bentley and Hansch (1969); its shape is Kubinyi's bilinear equation.
