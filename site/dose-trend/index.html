How a trend test reads a dose table

Headache was reported by 31% of patients on placebo and 62% on the highest dose of apricitabine. Is that the drug, or 13 patients having an unlucky ten days? A trend test answers by measuring how far the cases lean toward the high doses, then asking how often pure chance leans that far.

Five small groups

Every patient in this trial was assigned at random to one group and took that dose for ten days, so the five groups are five different sets of people. Take the highlighted row. In the figure each dot is a patient, and a filled dot is a patient who reported headache.

Groups this size are coarse instruments. In a group of 13, one patient is worth 7.7 percentage points, and the whole gap between placebo and the top dose is 4 patients.

What chance does to small groups

Suppose the dose made no difference at all. The 25 patients who got a headache would have got one anyway, and which group each of them landed in was settled by the randomization. You can replay that draw: keep the 25 cases, and deal them out again at random among the 63 patients.

Why 25 and not 21? The table’s Total column adds up the four apricitabine groups only. With placebo counted as well, 25 of the 63 patients reported a headache.

Chance alone builds staircases, cliffs and spikes. Counting every possible deal, the top dose ends up at least 4 patients ahead of placebo in 8.1% of them. So the useful question is never whether there is a pattern. It is whether the pattern is larger than shuffling usually makes.

One number for the whole staircase

Comparing only the two end groups throws away the three in between. A trend test uses all five. Picture a beam marked in milligrams, with every patient who had a headache standing at their dose. If dose were irrelevant, the cases would balance at the average dose of all 63 patients. If they crowd toward the high doses, their balance point moves right.

That distance is the trend statistic. Cochran in 1954 and Armitage in 1955 wrote it as the sum of dose × cases; dividing by the number of cases turns it into the lean you see here. In the trial the cases averaged 976 mg against 819 mg for everyone, a lean of 157 mg.

How far does chance lean?

Now shuffle again, thousands of times, and keep only the lean from each shuffle.

The share of shuffles in the tails is the P value. Counting every possible shuffle gives P = 0.090. The Cochran–Armitage test swaps the pile for a bell curve and reports P = 0.077 (z = 1.77). With groups of 11 to 14 the bell is a slightly generous fit, so the exact figure is the safer one to quote. Either way a lean this large turns up about one time in 11 when dose does nothing.

One question or four

A common alternative is to compare each dose with placebo, one pair at a time. That asks four questions of 24 to 27 patients each, and the P values have to be adjusted because there are four of them. The trend test asks one ordered question of all 63.

Why not McNemar’s test? McNemar’s is for paired data, the same patients measured twice. Here each group is a different set of patients, so the pairwise step uses Fisher’s exact test.

The two approaches answer different questions and can disagree in both directions. The staircase gives a trend with P = 0.022 while no dose on its own gets below 0.11. The spike gives a pairwise P of 0.012 after adjustment while the trend test sees nothing (P = 0.35), because one ordered question is blind to anything that is not a climb.

One patient from a different verdict

Here is the uncomfortable part. Hold the middle groups as they were and vary only the two ends: the number of cases on placebo and at the top dose.

One case fewer on placebo and the same test reports P = 0.031. One more at the top dose: P = 0.035. The exact versions agree (0.038 and 0.041). “Not significant” is a statement about these 63 patients, not about apricitabine. The honest summary is the estimate with its uncertainty: +30.8 percentage points at the top dose, 95% confidence interval −6.6 to 58.3.

What the test leaves unsaid

Three limits are worth carrying away. The scores are a choice: using the doses in milligrams assumes each step matters in proportion to its size, and equally spaced scores would be just as defensible. The test looks for a climb and nothing else, so a spike or a plateau can hide from it, which is why Figure 7 also reports the departure from a straight line. And this table has nine rows. Test each one at 0.05 and, if the rows were independent and the drug did nothing, the chance that at least one dips under the line is 37%.

None of that makes the test less useful. It makes the single number less important than the picture around it: how big the difference is, how wide the interval, and how few patients stand between one verdict and the other.
